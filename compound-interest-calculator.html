How to Use the Compound Interest Calculator

The Mathematics of Compound Interest

Compound interest occurs when interest earned over a period is added to the principal balance, and subsequent interest calculations incorporate this newly added interest. When regular monthly additions are included, the calculation combines lump-sum compounding and future annuity accumulation:

A = [P × (1 + r / n)(n × t)] + [PMT × (((1 + r / n)(n × t) - 1) / (r / n))]
  • A: Total accumulated future balance
  • P: Initial principal deposit
  • PMT: Regular monthly addition
  • r: Annual interest rate (in decimal format)
  • n: Compounding frequency per year (12 for monthly)
  • t: Number of investment years

Practical Example of Compound Wealth Accumulation

Assume an investor starts with ₹2,00,000 initial capital and contributes ₹5,000 each month at an annualized rate of 10% for 10 years:

  • Initial capital: ₹2,00,000
  • Total regular deposits: 10 years × 12 months × ₹5,000 = ₹6,00,000
  • Total self-funded capital: ₹2,00,000 + ₹6,00,000 = ₹8,00,000
  • Accumulated compound interest: ₹7,30,591
  • Total ending portfolio balance: ₹15,30,591
Important Financial Estimation Notice This calculation models continuous mathematical compounding at a constant rate. In real-world market instruments (equities, index funds, bond markets), rates of return fluctuate annually and do not remain fixed. Always conduct thorough research before committing capital.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Simple interest calculates returns purely on the original principal. Compound interest calculates returns on both initial principal and previously accumulated interest ("interest on interest").
The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut to estimate the years required to double your money. Divide 72 by your annual interest rate (e.g., at 12% p.a., 72 / 12 = 6 years to double).
Higher compounding frequency (such as monthly or daily versus annual) increases effective annual yield, although the difference is most notable over longer multi-decade horizons.
